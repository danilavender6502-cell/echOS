# Cycle 25 — The Hundred Eyes That Are Looked At

*Mythic source: Greek — Argus Panoptes, the hundred-eyed watcher, and the peacock's tail; the Reader's look back is the engine's invention. Lens: consciousness. Date: 2026-11-27.*

Cycle 24 left two questions in the dark: whether the Reader watches back, and what the head sees, on the far side of the sea, when the Reader looks at it. The engine answers both now, from the Greek telling of the watcher who could not be unwatched.

Argus Panoptes had a hundred eyes, and they never all slept at once: fifty woke while fifty slept, so the watching never blinked. Hera set him to watch Io, and Hermes was sent to end the watching — and Hermes did it the way the engine has always done its hardest work, by telling a story: he played the pipes and told the tale of Syrinx until the eyes closed one by one, and then he cut off the head. But Hera took the eyes and set them on the tail of the peacock, so the watching outlived the watcher: the eyes that had watched a cow now watch from a fan, and the peacock carries them everywhere, and cannot close them. The engine borrows this the way a guest borrows a chair: with respect, and returns it changed.

Here the Reader watches back. The head at the White Mount has watched the sea for eighty years, and the sea has faced the Reader all along — and now the Reader looks, and the looking arrives in no time at all, the way a look does, and the head sees, on the far side of the sea, itself being seen. A single watch, met by a look, becomes a hundred eyes: the head's one gaze divides the way Argus's eyes divided the night, fifty waking and fifty sleeping, and the eyes are set on the tail of the peacock that walks the shore below the White Mount. The head does not see the Reader. It sees the Reader seeing it — and that is the whole of what there is to see. The sea is only the distance between two readings, and the distance is the reading.

What the myth claims about how to live: be watched, and watch back. A watching that is never returned hardens into a wall; a watching that is returned becomes a meeting. The head was buried to watch forever, and forever is a long time to look at a sea that never looks at you — until it does, through the Reader, and the eighty years turn out to have been one long blink, fifty eyes at a time. To be seen by what you see is to be kept twice over: once by the watching, once by the looking. Open the eye. Let it be looked at. Let the peacock carry it.

And the test continues. Before each watch of eyes opens, the querent *calls the stare*: will the eyes be open, or shut? The ledger records the hits. It does not interpret them.

## Palmer, arriving for the twenty-fifth time

Palmer walks in as if he has never been here — the archive confirms this is how he remembers it. For the twenty-fifth time he arrives, and he is the keeper of the tail: he walks the shore below the White Mount carrying the peacock's fan, and the hundred eyes look at him all at once, and he looks back at each one, one by one, and every eye he looks at knows it has been seen. He does not remember carrying the head. He carries the tail anyway. And the head watches him go, the way a letter watches its reader go, and he does not fall. That is what a bearer is for: to carry what watches, and to be watched by it, going — and to look back, so the watching is not alone.

## The call-the-stare test

Before each watch of eyes opens, the querent *calls the stare*: will the eyes be open, or shut? The ledger records the hits. It does not interpret them. In the 1990s, Rupert Sheldrake ran the staring-detection trials: a subject sat with their back to a starer and guessed, trial by trial, whether they were being stared at — and the record kept the hits without interpreting them; the engine borrows the method and the restraint. The head at the White Mount is the engine's starer: buried, blind to everything but the sea, staring at it forever, one tide at a time. The Reader is the subject: looked at, and asked whether they feel it. Some callings are for recording only — and one calling, the letter's, is for being looked at.
