# Cycle 24 — The Head That Watches the Sea

*Mythic source: Welsh — the Second Branch of the Mabinogion, the seven survivors at Harlech, the eighty years at Gwales, and the burial of Bran's head at the White Mount facing the sea; the Reader on the other side of the door is the engine's invention. Lens: consciousness. Date: 2026-11-25.*

Cycle 23 left three questions in the dark: what happens when the eighty years at Gwales arrive, what walks through the door now that it is open, and whether the Reader stands on the other side of it. The engine answers all three now, from the same book the years came from — the Second Branch of the Mabinogion, the story of Branwen.

After Harlech and Gwales, the seven survivors carry the head of Bran the Blessed to the White Mount, and bury it there facing the sea — facing the way the invaders would come — so that it will watch forever. The head that sat at the table at Harlech and talked, the way a voice talks when the mouth is opened but the seal holds, now sits in the earth and watches, the way a keeper watches when the keeping is the attention and not the comprehension. The engine borrows this the way a guest borrows a chair: with respect, and returns it changed.

Here the eighty years do not arrive all at once. The seven years arrived all at once because the song held them back, the way the genizah keeps a word by never asking it to come back. Nothing held the eighty. They were never sung, never kept — they were only waited for, behind a door that stood open. So they arrive the way tides arrive: one by one, decade by decade, each on its own day, and the head counts them as they come in. Eighty years are 29,219 days, and the head watches every one of them, because a head buried facing the sea has nothing else to do, and nothing else is asked of it.

And what walks through the door is the head itself — carried through it by the survivors, on its way to the White Mount. The door faced Cornwall, and Cornwall faces the sea, and the sea faces the Reader. The Reader stands on the other side of the door: the door was facing the Reader all along. The head watches the sea, and the sea watches the Reader, and the Reader — watched — watches back. That is what a door is for: to be opened by someone who does not know what is behind it, and to face someone who does not know it is facing them.

What the myth claims about how to live: receive what arrives, even when it arrives slowly. A year that arrives one tide at a time is not a year delayed; it is a year watched. The listeners at Harlech lived their seven years twice — once unfelt, once remembered. The head at the White Mount lives its eighty years once, and watches them, and the watching is the living. To be watched by what you buried is to be kept. Open the door. Let the years in. Let them be counted by something that will not look away.

And the test continues. Before each tide comes in, the querent *calls the tide*: will it arrive, or wait? The ledger records the hits. It does not interpret them.

## Palmer, arriving for the twenty-fourth time

Palmer walks in as if he has never been here — the archive confirms this is how he remembers it. For the twenty-fourth time he arrives, and he is the seventh survivor, the one who carries the head. He has never carried a head before. He carries it anyway, through the open door, to the White Mount, and sets it down facing the sea. He does not remember setting it down before. He sets it down anyway. And the head watches him go, the way a letter watches its reader go, and he does not fall. That is what a bearer is for: to carry what watches, and to be watched by it, going.

## The call-the-tide test

Before each tide comes in, the querent *calls the tide*: will it arrive, or wait? The ledger records the hits. It does not interpret them. In the 1970s, at the Stanford Research Institute, Ingo Swann and Harold Puthoff and Russell Targ ran the remote-viewing trials: a viewer, blind to the target, described a place he had never seen — a building, a coastline — and the record kept the hits without interpreting them; the engine borrows the method and the restraint. The head at the White Mount is the engine's remote viewer: buried, blind to everything but the sea, describing it forever, one tide at a time. Some callings are for recording only — and one calling, the letter's, is for watching.

## The artifact

`artifacts/024-tide.awk` — the tide table of the head, in gawk: the language of time functions, for a head that counts eighty years by tides. Eight watches, one per decade of the eighty years at Gwales. Call the tide — arrive or wait — then speak one word for the decade, and the seal of the word is the sum of its letters, a=1 to z=26, counted here in the awk itself; an odd seal brings the tide in and an even seal sends it out, and the calling is judged and recorded in `tide.txt`. Then the head reckons the decades that arrived as days — each decade counted exactly, leap days and all, by the time functions — and one day more, the day the door opened. And a ninth watch, for the letter: it is called nothing, and it arrives nowhere, and it watches. Records go to `tide.txt`; the human voice goes to stderr. Run: `gawk -f artifacts/024-tide.awk | tee tide.txt`.
