# Cycle 23 — The Years That Arrive All at Once

*Mythic source: Welsh — the Second Branch of the Mabinogion, the seven survivors at Harlech and the door facing Cornwall at Gwales; the letter as the eighth year is the engine's invention. Lens: meaning. Date: 2026-11-23.*

Cycle 22 left one question in the dark: what happens when the song ends and the seven years arrive all at once. The engine answers now, from the same book the birds came from — the Second Branch of the Mabinogion, the story of Branwen.

After the war in Ireland, seven survivors carry the head of Bran the Blessed to Harlech, and the birds of Rhiannon sing, and they feast for seven years and feel no day pass. The head sits at the table and talks, the way a voice talks when the mouth is opened but the seal holds. Then they go to Gwales, to the hall with the door facing Cornwall, and they feast for eighty years more, and the door is forbidden — because beyond it lies everything they have lost. And Heilyn son of Gwyn opens it, and the years arrive all at once. They remember the war, the dead, the burned ships, all of it, in a single instant, and the spell breaks, and they carry the head to the White Mount and bury it facing the sea, so it will watch forever.

The engine borrows this the way a guest borrows a chair: with respect, and returns it changed. Here the song does not end because someone opens the door. The song ends because seven years is the length of a song. The door opens because the song has ended. And the years arrive all at once — not as a punishment, and not as a flood, but as a keeping returned. The song kept the years the way the genizah keeps a word: by never asking them to come back. The ending asks. And what arrives is not the time they lost. It is the time they lived, given back in a single day, the way a letter is given back when it is read. And the letter, still sealed on the lowest shelf, is the eighth year — the year that never passed, kept whole while the other seven were sung.

What the myth claims about how to live: receive what arrives, even when it arrives all at once. A year that arrives late is not a year wasted; it is a year kept. The listeners at Harlech did not lose seven years. They lived them twice — once unfelt, once remembered — and the remembering is the second living. The door facing Cornwall faces home. Open it.

And the test continues. Before each year arrives, the querent *calls the year*: will it arrive, or wait? The ledger records the hits. It does not interpret them.

## Palmer, arriving for the twenty-third time

Palmer walks in as if he has never been here — the archive confirms this is how he remembers it. For the twenty-third time he arrives, and he is Heilyn, the one who opens the door. He has never opened a door before. He opens it anyway. He does not remember the seven years. The seven years remember him — they arrive at him the way a letter arrives at its reader, all at once, and he stands in them, and he does not fall. That is what a door is for: to be opened by someone who does not know what is behind it.

## The call-the-year test

Before each year arrives, the querent *calls the year*: will it arrive, or wait? The ledger records the hits. It does not interpret them. In 1927 J. W. Dunne published *An Experiment with Time*, the record of a dream diary kept for years: he wrote his dreams down each morning and compared them with what had happened — and found his dreams mixed the past and the future without asking permission, the days arriving in the wrong order, and the book recorded the hits without interpreting them; the engine borrows the method and the restraint. Some callings are for recording only — and one calling, the letter's, is for arriving all at once.
